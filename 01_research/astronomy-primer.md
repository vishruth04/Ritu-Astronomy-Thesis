# Astronomy primer for the exhibits

Status: Draft, 27 Nov 2026. The science content behind each gallery, written so it can go straight onto exhibit labels, sheets and the report. Facts marked **(K)** are standard textbook values from general knowledge; check them against a textbook or NASA fact sheet before printing. Kodaikanal-specific facts come from the site research file.

## 1. Why astronomy belongs in Kodaikanal

- Kodaikanal sits at about **10.23° N, 77.47° E**, at about 2,133-2,343 m. Thin, clean, dry-season air above the haze of the plains is why the British chose it for a solar observatory in 1899.
- The **Kodaikanal Solar Observatory (KSO)** has more than 100 years of daily images of the Sun, one of the longest continuous solar records in the world (about 200,000 plates and films).
- In January 1909 John Evershed discovered here the **Evershed effect**: gas flowing outward across the dark centre of a sunspot. It is the most important discovery made from Indian soil in that era, and a perfect story for the Sun Gallery.
- The museum's job: turn that scientific heritage, which today is mostly shown with posters, into spaces people can walk through.

## 2. The Sun (Sun Gallery)

| Fact | Value (K) | Label idea |
|---|---|---|
| Diameter | about 1.39 million km (109 Earths across) | "109 Earths would fit across the Sun." |
| Distance from Earth | about 150 million km (1 astronomical unit, AU) | "Sunlight you feel now left the Sun 8 minutes 20 seconds ago." |
| Surface temperature | about 5,500 °C | |
| Core temperature | about 15 million °C | |
| Age | about 4.6 billion years | |
| Composition | about 73% hydrogen, 25% helium by mass | |
| Rotation | about 25 days at the equator, about 35 near the poles | KSO's century of sunspot records shows this |
| Sunspot cycle | about 11 years | A floor timeline of 125 years of KSO sunspot counts |

**Key ideas to show**

1. **Sunspots** are cooler, darker patches where magnetic fields block heat. They look black only because the rest of the Sun is brighter: chiaroscuro on the Sun itself.
2. **The Evershed effect** (KSO, 1909): gas flows outward along the penumbra (the lighter ring) of a sunspot at about 1-2 km/s (K).
3. **Never look at the Sun directly.** Safe ways the gallery can show it: projecting the Sun's image onto a white screen (as a coelostat or a telescope with a projection screen does), a pinhole/camera-obscura room, or certified solar filters at a supervised telescope.
4. **The Sun's daily and yearly path** (for the sundial court and oculus):
   - Noon Sun height in Kodaikanal (K, from 90° - latitude ± 23.44°): about **80° to the south at the equinoxes**, about **56° to the south at the December solstice**, about **77° to the north at the June solstice**.
   - **Zero Shadow Day:** at latitude 10.2° N the noon Sun is exactly overhead twice a year, around **mid-April (about 16-17 April)** and **late August (about 26-27 August)** (K, verify the exact dates for the year). A vertical pole casts no shadow at noon. This is a powerful, free, "the building is the exhibit" moment: an oculus can drop a disc of light onto a marked floor spot on exactly those days.

## 3. Light and the spectrum (Spectrum Chamber)

- White light is a mix of colours. A prism bends each colour by a different amount and spreads them into a **spectrum** (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet).
- The Sun's spectrum is crossed by thin dark lines, the **Fraunhofer lines** (K). Each set of lines is the fingerprint of an element (hydrogen, sodium, calcium, iron) absorbing its own colours. That is how we know what the Sun and stars are made of without going there.
- KSO has photographed the Sun in single colours for over a century (calcium K and hydrogen-alpha light), so the Spectrum Chamber can show real KSO images in each colour.
- **Room idea:** one white beam enters a dark, mirror-lined chamber, passes through a large prism or dichroic glass, and paints the walls with colour. A rotating disc of glass shifts the colours as visitors walk. This is the brightest colour in a darkening building.
- **Label line:** "Every colour has a name tag. Starlight carries the names of the atoms that made it."

## 4. The Moon (Moon gallery)

| Fact | Value (K) |
|---|---|
| Diameter | about 3,474 km (about a quarter of Earth) |
| Distance | about 384,400 km on average |
| Cycle of phases | about 29.5 days (new Moon to new Moon) |
| Same face always toward Earth | because it turns once on its axis for each orbit ("tidal locking") |
| Light | the Moon makes no light; it reflects sunlight |

**Key ideas to show**

1. **Phases** are pure chiaroscuro: one lamp (the Sun) lights one half of a ball (the Moon); as we walk around it, we see more or less of the lit half. Build this literally: a single strong lamp at one end of a dark room and white spheres around a circle that visitors walk past.
2. **Craters show best at the terminator** (the line between day and night on the Moon), where raking light throws long shadows. A tactile relief Moon lit from a low angle shows this; visually impaired visitors can feel the same craters.
3. **Eclipses:** the Moon is about 400 times smaller than the Sun but about 400 times closer, so they look the same size in our sky (K). That coincidence is why total solar eclipses happen.
4. Indian connections: the Tamil and Hindu calendars are lunar-solar; festivals follow the Moon (Pournami/full Moon, Amavasai/new Moon). Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023 (K), now National Space Day in India.

### Scale corridor numbers (worked example)

The corridor in the area programme is about 120 m², roughly 40 m long and 3 m wide. If Neptune is placed at 36 m from the Sun:

- **1 AU = 1.2 m**, so the scale is about **1 : 125 billion**.
- Positions from the Sun marker: Mercury 0.47 m, Venus 0.86 m, Earth 1.20 m, Mars 1.83 m, Jupiter 6.24 m, Saturn 11.4 m, Uranus 23.0 m, Neptune 36.1 m.
- At the same scale the Sun is a bead about **11 mm** across and Earth a speck about **0.1 mm** across. This is the point of the corridor: space is almost entirely empty.
- At this scale the nearest star, Proxima Centauri (4.24 light-years), would be about **320 km away**, roughly the distance from Kodaikanal to Bengaluru in a straight line (K, check on a map). Put that line on the corridor's last wall.
- Design note: the inner planets crowd into the first 2 m. Either use a second, larger scale for planet sizes (shown as models in wall niches), or start the corridor with a "zoom" panel. Show both scales clearly; reviewers will ask.

## 6. Stars and deep space (Star chamber and dome)

- A **light-year** is the distance light travels in a year: about 9.46 trillion km (K).
- Stars are suns. Their colour shows their temperature: red stars are cooler (about 3,000 °C), blue stars hotter (above 10,000 °C). A colour-coded star ceiling teaches this with no text.
- **The Milky Way** is our galaxy, about 100,000 light-years across with 100-400 billion stars (K). From a dark site it looks like a band of light across the sky; most city people have never seen it. The star chamber recreates that sky.
- **What the sky above Kodaikanal shows** (latitude about 10° N):
  - The Pole Star (Polaris) sits only about 10° above the northern horizon.
  - Because it is close to the equator, Kodaikanal sees much of the southern sky too: the Southern Cross (Crux) and Alpha Centauri are visible low in the south in the early months of the year (about January to May, evening) (K).
  - Orion is high on winter evenings (December to February), and Scorpius with the Milky Way's centre in Sagittarius on evenings around June to August (K).
- **Indian sky traditions:** the 27 **nakshatras** (lunar mansions) along the Moon's path; Saptarishi for the Big Dipper; Mrigashira for part of Orion (K). A nakshatra ring in the star chamber links the science to local culture.
- **Why darkness matters (the concept):** we only see stars because the sky around them is dark. Light pollution hides about a third of humanity's view of the Milky Way (K, from the 2016 world atlas of artificial night-sky brightness; verify the number). The museum's dark interior and dark-sky exterior lighting make this argument in built form.

## 7. Indian astronomy story (timeline wall)

| When (K, verify) | What |
|---|---|
| About 5th century CE | Aryabhata proposes that Earth spins on its axis |
| 1720s-1730s | Sawai Jai Singh II builds the Jantar Mantar observatories (Jaipur, Delhi, Ujjain, Varanasi, Mathura) |
| 1786/1792 | Madras Observatory, Chennai; the forerunner of KSO |
| 1899 | Kodaikanal Solar Observatory founded |
| 1909 | Evershed effect discovered at KSO |
| 1975 | Aryabhata, India's first satellite |
| 2014 | Mangalyaan reaches Mars orbit |
| 2023 | Chandrayaan-3 lands near the Moon's south pole; Aditya-L1 solar mission launched |

The Aditya-L1 link closes a loop: India's first space solar observatory (2023) continues what KSO started on this hill in 1899. Ideal final panel for the Sun Gallery.

## 8. Writing good exhibit labels

- 50-75 words per label, Tamil and English; one idea per label; a question or action in the first line ("Put your hand here. This is how hot...").
- Use comparisons people can feel (sizes in Earths, distances in travel times).
- Keep numbers rounded on labels; keep exact numbers and sources in the report.
